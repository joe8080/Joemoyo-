## 2. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Mansa Musa I (c.1280 – c.1337) was the ninth — or possibly tenth — mansa of Mali, ruling from about 1312. He is the best-documented sub-Saharan African sovereign of the medieval period, and the only one whose wealth was recorded in writing by the bureaucracy of a rival superpower.

His significance does not rest on the internet's favourite claim. The database is unambiguous that "richest person in history" is a modern media construct with no medieval source behind it, and that the $400 billion figure traces to a 2012 celebrity-net-worth blog. OGX covers Musa **in spite of** that claim, not because of it.

Three things survive the evidence gate:

1. **An economic footprint recorded by outsiders.** Al-Umari, writing in Cairo from eyewitness informants, reports the Egyptian gold price stayed depressed for roughly a decade after 1324. A West African state moved the bullion market of the Mamluk Sultanate.
2. **Institutions that outlived him.** The Djinguereber Mosque (1327) and the Sankoré complex. Buildings, not anecdotes.
3. **Diplomatic visibility in Europe.** In 1375 a Majorcan Jewish cartographer, working from Saharan trade intelligence, placed Musa at the centre of the most important map of the medieval world — crowned, enthroned, captioned as the richest and noblest king in the land. Fifty years after his death, a century before any European reached West Africa.

That last point is the spine of the treatment. Europe was not ignorant of African power. It mapped it, then chose to forget.

## 3. HISTORICAL CONTEXT

The Mali Empire (c.1226 – c.1670) was a Mandinka-founded state on the upper Niger, succeeding Ghana/Wagadu and preceding Songhai. Its wealth had a specific mechanism. The trans-Saharan gold-salt network (c.300 – c.1600, peak c.800–1400) ran on complementarity: West Africa had gold and no salt; the Sahara had salt and no gold; the Mediterranean had an unlimited appetite for gold coinage. Crossings took sixty to seventy days. Mali held the goldfields of Bambuk and Bure *and* the salt at Taghaza — both ends of the exchange.

Musa's world was not isolated. Mali maintained relations with Morocco, Egypt and the Mamluk Sultanate, and the hajj route through Cairo was a diplomatic corridor as much as a religious one. The Arabic chancery tradition that recorded him was the record-keeping apparatus of a rival great power.

The constitutional inheritance matters too. The Kouroukan Fouga, attributed to Sundiata c.1235, established provincial governance under *farba*, a three-tier citizenship structure, the political role of the griots, and protections for women, children and certain trees — recognised by UNESCO in 2009. Musa inherited an administrative machine, not a warband.

## 4. CHRONOLOGICAL NARRATIVE

**Formation (c.1235 – c.1312).** Mali is founded after Kirina, in a tradition transmitted by griots and written down centuries later. Every element of this phase is flagged contested — the epic is magnificent evidence of Mande historical memory and weak evidence of specific dates.

**Accession (c.1312).** Musa comes to power after his predecessor's departure. The database disagrees with itself on the name: the Musa record says "Abu Bakr II"; the dedicated record states flatly that this is a nineteenth-century mistranslation and that Ibn Khaldun calls him Muhammad ibn Qu. On screen: "Musa's predecessor", unnamed.

**Rise (c.1312 – 1324).** Mali reaches roughly 1.29 million km², spanning modern Mali, Senegal, the Gambia, Guinea and parts of Mauritania and Niger, with routes to Cairo, Fez, Tlemcen and Tunis.

**Peak — the hajj (1324–1325).** What the sources support: a very large entourage, exceptional expenditure, gifts to the Mamluk court recorded independently by al-Maqrizi, and a gold-price depression al-Umari puts at ten to twelve years. What they do not support: headcounts. Ibn Khaldun's figures carry a contested flag; al-Umari's are classed as rhetorical.

**Return and construction (1325 – c.1337).** Musa returns with al-Sahili and commissions Djinguereber, completed 1327. Timbuktu begins its transformation from Tuareg seasonal camp to intellectual centre — a process that *peaks under Songhai, not under Musa*, and the distinction must hold on screen.

**Death (c.1337, disputed).** Three dates circulate: 1332, 1337, 1341.

**Aftermath.** Ibn Battuta reaches Mali in 1352–53 and describes Mansa Sulayman's court, citing Musa's administration as precedent. In 1375 the Catalan Atlas fixes Musa in European cartography. Mali is eclipsed by Songhai; Timbuktu's scholarly infrastructure is broken by the Moroccan invasion of 1591 and the sack of 1593, when Ahmad Baba is deported to Marrakech.

## 7. CONTESTED CLAIMS

**7.1 "Richest person in history."** *A:* his wealth exceeds any documented fortune. *B:* pre-modern and modern wealth are not commensurable, and no medieval source makes the claim. *DB consensus:* modern media construct. **Never assert.** If used, frame as "a claim that circulates online — and that historians reject." Note the debate row itself is thin: `position_a`, `position_b`, `key_scholars` are all null.

**7.2 Did the hajj crash Egypt's economy?** *A:* popular framing — collapse for a decade. *B:* al-Umari describes a depressed *gold price*, and is the only surviving source blaming Musa for a twelve-year decline. *DB:* the person record calls "crashed the Egyptian economy" exaggerated; the event records repeating "crashed" carry the lowest confidence in the set (33 and 53). *WEB corroboration:* Warren Schultz's reappraisal finds support for a local devaluation around 1324 but not a sustained national collapse. **On screen:** "According to al-Umari, the price of gold in Egypt remained depressed for roughly a decade."

**7.3 Caravan numbers.** 60,000 attendants, 12,000 slaves, camels at 300 pounds of gold each (Ibn Khaldun); 500 gold-staff bearers (al-Umari). Both flagged as second-hand, recorded fifty-plus years later, plausibly Mali-court self-reporting relayed through Egyptian intermediaries. **Attribute by name every time and say the figures are rhetorical.**

**7.4 Regnal number.** Ninth versus tenth — unresolved internal DB conflict. Avoid the ordinal, or state that the king-lists disagree.

**7.5 Death date.** 1332, 1337, 1341. *WEB:* Levtzion favours 1337; back-calculating Ibn Khaldun's reign lengths from Sulayman's death in 1360 yields 1332. On screen: "c.1337 — the sources disagree."

**7.6 "Abubakari II" and the Atlantic.** *A:* a mansa launched a fleet c.1311 and never returned; Van Sertima argued it reached the Americas. *B:* one uncorroborated second-hand anecdote in al-Umari; the name appears nowhere in it — a nineteenth-century mistranslation by Baron de Slane. Ibn Khaldun, Ibn Battuta and the Tarikh al-fattash mention no voyage at all. *DB:* the pre-Columbian claim has "zero archaeological, genetic, or documentary support." **Usable as an episode about medieval African maritime ambition and about how a translation error became a myth. Not as an African discovery of America.**

**7.7 Two-thirds of Old World gold.** Flagged contested in the DB's own records — export estimates vary widely. Attribute as an estimate.

**7.8 Timbuktu's scale.** Three manuscript figures in the DB. The 25,000-student figure attaches to **1500 under Songhai**, and the Songhai record explicitly warns of popular exaggeration. Do not transplant Songhai numbers onto Musa.

**7.9 Niani as capital.** "Traditionally identified", conf. 48. Say "traditionally identified as Niani."

**7.10 The founding tradition.** All three Sundiata/Kirina rows carry contested flags; the Tarikh al-fattash is a seventeenth-century reconstruction of a thirteenth-century event. Attribute to the griot tradition explicitly.

## 8. THE IDENTITY / SUPPRESSION ANGLE

**What was erased.** Not Musa the man — he survived, flattened into a listicle. What was erased was the *system*: a bureaucratic, constitutional, literate West African state with provincial governors, Islamic courts, a manuscript economy and diplomatic standing in Cairo, Fez and Tunis. Reducing that to one anecdote about a king giving away gold *is* the erasure. It converts an empire into a curiosity.

**By whom, in three documented phases:**

1. **European forgetting, from a position of prior knowledge.** The Catalan Atlas settles this. A Majorcan Jewish cartographer, using Arab trade intelligence, drew Musa crowned and enthroned and told the courts of Europe exactly where the gold came from and who controlled it. The archive's own framing: this "proves Europe was not ignorant of African civilisational power. It chose not to engage as an equal." The later doctrine of a dark, historyless continent was not inherited ignorance — it was a reversal.
2. **Physical destruction of the scholarly record.** The invasion of 1591 and the sack of 1593 broke the institutional continuity of West African learning. Ahmad Baba — author of over forty works, and of a legal opinion against the enslavement of Muslim Africans — was arrested in 1594 and deported to Marrakech. Scholars removed; manuscripts scattered into private family libraries.
3. **Knowledge extraction.** Leo Africanus is the clearest case: a Moroccan diplomat captured, forcibly baptised, renamed by a pope, set to writing the definitive European account of his own continent — describing Timbuktu's universities and manuscript trade for a publishing world that was financing slave voyages. African civilisation was documented, then ignored when the documentation became inconvenient.

**How it was recovered.** By the people who never let go of it. The griots — hereditary *jeli*, trained from childhood — held the Mande record across eight centuries without a state to protect it. Timbuktu's families kept manuscripts in domestic libraries through four centuries of disruption. In 2012–13, under Ansar Dine occupation, Abdel Kader Haidara and the city's librarians moved roughly 350,000 manuscripts to Bamako by car, donkey and boat. UNESCO recognised Timbuktu in 1988 and the Kouroukan Fouga in 2009.

**The closing turn.** Mali's 2023 mining code raised state equity to 35 per cent and recovered $1.2 billion in back taxes; in 2025 the state extracted a $430 million settlement from Barrick Gold. The gold under Mali is still the argument. Musa's descendants are not litigating a legend — they are litigating an extraction contract.
