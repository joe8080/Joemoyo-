## 2. DESCRIPTION (paste into YouTube description box)

In 25 BCE an African queen led her army into Roman Egypt, sacked its frontier cities, and took the bronze head of a statue of the emperor Augustus. She carried it 800 miles south and buried it beneath a temple threshold at her capital — so that every worshipper would tread the face of Rome's first emperor underfoot, forever.

Her name was Amanirenas. Her kingdom was Meroë — the southern, Meroitic-speaking successor to the Kingdom of Kush, on the Nile in what is now central Sudan. From around 270 BCE to 350 CE it was an independent African state: over 200 pyramids, the oldest indigenous writing system in Africa south of the Sahara, an iron industry rivalling Rome's, and a line of ruling queens — the kandakes — who commanded armies in their own name.

When Augustus sent three legions south under Petronius, Rome could raid Meroë but could not conquer it. In 21 BCE the Treaty of Samos withdrew the Roman frontier, exempted Meroë from tribute, and — by the ancient sources — had Rome pay to keep the peace. It held for nearly 300 years. Meroë finally fell not to Rome but to the rising African empire of Aksum, around 350 CE.

This is the African superpower Western world-history syllabi left out.
