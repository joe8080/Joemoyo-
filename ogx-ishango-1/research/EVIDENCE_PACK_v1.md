## 1. Topic question
What do we actually know about the notched bone from Ishango (DR Congo), and what is only interpretation? In particular: when it dates from, how many marks it has, and whether those marks are numbers, a calendar, or something else.

## 2. Documented vs debated (summary)
DOCUMENTED (from the holding institution and other sources):
1. Site: Ishango, on the Congolese shore of Lake Edward, in what is now DR Congo (C01). Hubert Damas found the site in 1935 (C04).
2. Excavated by Belgian geologist Jean de Heinzelin. The museum says 1950; the brochure gives excavations in 1950 and 1959 (C02, C03).
3. Held by the Institute of Natural Sciences (formerly RBINS) in Brussels (C05).
4. Object: a bone handle about 10 cm long with a fragment of quartz at one end, probably a tool (C06).
5. Bone type: a mammal bone. The museum says the species can no longer be told. "Baboon fibula" is NOT documented (C07).
6. Marks: 168 notches in three columns: 11, 13, 17, 19 (60) | 3, 6, 4, 8, 10, 5, 5, 7 (48) | 11, 21, 19, 9 (60). Some marks are worn, and a 10 may be a 9 (C08, C09).
7. The people who lived there were lakeside fishers who made bone harpoons. Remains of at least nine people were found (C18, C19).
DEBATED (interpretation):
1. Arithmetic game, doubling and primes: de Heinzelin, 1962 (D01, D02).
2. Counting tool mixing bases 10 and 12, called "oldest mathematical tool": Pletser & Huylebrouck (D03).
3. Lunar calendar: Marshack. Now largely rejected (D04).
4. Tally of some count: Smithsonian, "may" (D05).
5. Not necessarily numbers at all: Keller (D06).
6. Purpose of the quartz: engraving, tattooing or scarification (D07).

## 4. Dating: why the figures differ
1. Museum web page: "nearly 20,000 years old".
2. The same institution's 2024 brochure: carbon-14 found "several layers that are over 20,000 years old, the oldest of which are 25,000 years old", and it later says "it's 25,000 years old".
3. Smithsonian: "About 25,000 years old". This is the top of the range.
4. Pletser & Huylebrouck (2016): "22,000 years", by carbon-14, "as explained by Alison Brooks".
5. Keller: "usually dated to 20,000 years BCE", which is about 22,000 years ago.
6. Brooks & Smith (1987): the abstract only says "late Pleistocene". Their numeric ages (amino-acid racemization on shells) could not be read because the article is paywalled, so they are UNVERIFIED.
7. De Heinzelin's original 1950s-60s dating was far younger (secondary sources give about 9,000-6,500 BC; Cabinet says "11,000-year-old"). It was revised upwards after the 1980s re-study. UNVERIFIED from the primary text.
Explanation: the bone itself has not been directly dated. All the figures date the layers at the site. Sources round differently: some quote the oldest layer (25,000), some a mid figure (22,000), and some a rounded "about 20,000". The museum is not consistent with itself.
SAFE NARRATION: "around 20,000 years old" or "roughly 20,000 to 25,000 years old". Attribute one exact number to its source if you use it.

## 5. Notch counts: verified?
Yes, from institutional and scholarly sources: brochure p.19 (168 marks; 60/48/60); Pletser & Huylebrouck p.17 (same groups and sums); de Heinzelin 1962 as quoted by Keller pp.4-5. The arithmetic checks out (60 + 48 + 60 = 168).
Limit: I could not count the marks independently, because no open high-resolution image exists. Keller p.5 says "one of the sets of 5 is genuinely illegible, and in reality the 10 could be a 9". P&H mark these groups "10 (9+1 ?)" and "5 (1+4 ?)".
Narrate as "the museum counts 168 marks".

## 6. Disputed points (both readings)
1. Numbers or not?
   For: de Heinzelin 1962: "it seems probable that they were deliberately planned... an arithmetical game".
   Against: Keller p.4: "worn way [sic] or barely visible, which immediately makes any numeric interpretation suspect". Keller p.13: "A notch may be nothing more than a mark".
2. Primes?
   For: de Heinzelin noted 11, 13, 17, 19 are primes.
   Against: P&H "rejected... the one of prime numbers". The museum brochure says there is not enough proof.
3. Lunar calendar?
   For: Marshack (1964, 1972; not read first-hand), "related to the lunar calendar" as summarised by the brochure.
   Against: Keller "now largely discredited". d'Errico 1989 "A Case of Wishful Thinking?" (title only read).
4. "Oldest mathematical tool"?
   For: P&H abstract ("Our hypothesis, that is widely accepted"; "widely accepted" is their own self-description).
   Against: Keller says it was made famous "through mathematical illusion". Older notched bones also exist (Lebombo).
5. Decoration or grip?
   De Heinzelin suspected the marks were "more than pure decoration". No source I read argues for a grip.

2. De Heinzelin 1957 (Les fouilles d'Ishango) and the 1962 SciAm article in the original.

## 10. Do not say
1. "baboon fibula" (or any species).
2. "the oldest mathematical object", "proves Africans invented maths", "first calculator" as fact.
3. "lunar calendar" as fact.
4. "they knew prime numbers".
5. An exact date as settled fact, or "exactly 20,000/25,000 years".
6. "found in Egypt" or "near the source of the Nile" (the Claire H. caption is wrong).
7. "8500 BC" as the current date.
8. "hidden", "suppressed" or "lost knowledge" framing.
9. "the bone was carbon-dated" (the layers were dated, not the bone).
10. "Keller says it is just decoration" (he does not).
11. "widely accepted" for any reading.
12. "discovered in 1957/1960" (1957 is the publication year; the museum says 1950).
