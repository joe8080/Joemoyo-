L03	The style is called Nok, after a culture of central Nigeria.
L04	Its excavators describe Nok as the earliest large-scale sculptural tradition in sub-Saharan Africa.
L05	Yet they found no evidence of a state, of towns, or of an elite.
L06	So could small, scattered farming communities have made art on this scale?
L07	The first of these terracottas was noticed in 1928, during tin mining at the village of Nok.
L08	The British archaeologist Bernard Fagg collected the finds, and gave them a name: the Nok culture.
L09	Many of his pieces came from tin-mine gravels, moved from where they had first lain.
L10	A separate find came in 1943.

L12	It was brought to him in Jos.
L13	It is known as the Jemaa head, after the village where it was found.
L14	In the wake of Fagg's publications, Nok pieces flooded the international art market.
L15	Sites were dug out for sale, and the record of where things lay was lost.
L16	Later, archaeologists from Goethe University Frankfurt went looking.
L17	All of the team's first thirty sites had been looted.
L18	In their key study area, plundered sites dominate.
L19	Forgeries became a known problem too.
L20	Today, Nok terracottas are on the International Council of Museums' Red List of objects at risk.
L21	Very few Nok figures come from recorded excavations, and complete ones are extremely rare.
L22	That loss makes even a simple question hard. When?
L23	Britannica still describes Nok as existing between about 500 BCE and 200 CE.
L24	That range came from a handful of early radiocarbon and thermoluminescence dates.
L25	Museum records vary too: the Cleveland Museum of Art gives this head a thermoluminescence date of 20 to 620 CE.
L26	The Frankfurt team began fieldwork in 2005, and from 2009 its long-term project was funded by the German Research Foundation.
L27	They excavated, built a pottery sequence, and gathered more than two hundred radiocarbon dates.
L28	On their chronology, Nok began around 1500 BC.
L29	Its main phase ran from the ninth to the fourth century BC, when terracottas and iron appear.
L30	And it ended around the turn of the Common Era.
L31	The earliest furnace dates fall somewhere between 800 and 400 BC.
L32	But iron smelting by about 800 BC cannot yet be confirmed.
L33	So who made the figures?
L34	Art this accomplished, the thinking went, must have come from a complex society, with specialists to make it.
L35	Peter Breunig has said that, at first, his team assumed the same.
L36	What they found was smaller.
L37	Many sites cover roughly one or two hectares.
L38	They look like brief occupations, scattered across the land.
L39	No towns, no monuments and no central workshops have been found so far.
L40	Charred grains show what people grew: pearl millet, alongside cowpeas.
L41	Even the iron fits that picture: furnaces seem to have been used only once.
L42	The team reads that as a sign of travelling specialists, not a central workshop.
L43	As for iron objects, Breunig says everything they recovered fits into one hand.
L44	And the sculptures themselves turn up broken.
L45	Fragments lie in pits, and, as Breunig puts it, the pieces didn't fit together.
L46	The team suspects the figures were broken deliberately before burial.
L47	Gabriele Franke says they can't prove it, but the pattern is striking.
L48	At Pangwari, excavated between 2011 and 2014, they found a terracotta deposit in a secure archaeological context for the first time.
L49	The team interprets some deposits as graves, marked by upright stones in a row; no human remains survive.
L50	So what kind of society was this?
L51	Breunig and Rupp concluded that Nok developed complexity only in terms of ritual.
L52	They describe small groups of farmers, living autonomously in dispersed settlements.
L53	The excavators found no evidence of a state or an elite.
L54	That is not proof of absence: they stress that preservation is poor, and the data come from a limited area.
L55	There might have been rulers or chiefs, they write, who left no status markers.
L56	But on the evidence so far, Breunig says, highly accomplished art does not necessarily require a complex society.
L57	The work is unfinished.
L58	Fieldwork stopped after a deadly attack in 2017; the team has not gone back to the excavation area.
L59	Why Nok ended is still unknown; the team suggests environmental change as one possibility.
L60	Gabriele Franke puts it this way.
L61	"The 'mystery of the Nok culture' hasn't been solved – but today we have a much clearer understanding of what that mystery actually is."
L62	Great art – and, so far, no evidence of a great state.
