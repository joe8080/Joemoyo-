## 2. Clean narration (v0.1)
### R1 · The Letter
On the sixth of July, 1526, a letter left the royal city of Kongo, addressed to King João the Third of Portugal.
It was written down by a secretary, Dom João Teixeira, and signed by the king of Kongo. His Christian name was Afonso. In his own language, he was Mvemba a Nzinga.
It starts politely. A Portuguese ship has arrived at Soyo, on the coast. The church is short of wine and flour for the Mass. And he's sorry to hear that a Portuguese queen has died.
Then the tone changes.
"Our kingdom," he writes, "is being lost." Merchants are taking "our people, sons of the land, sons of our nobles and vassals, and our relatives." And the land, he says, is being emptied.

### R2 · The Short Answer
So why would a king in Central Africa write that to Portugal?
Here's the short answer. Afonso wasn't asking Portugal to end slavery. Slavery existed in Kongo, and Afonso himself sent war captives to Portuguese ships. What he objected to was free people being kidnapped and sold, by people he couldn't stop, in a trade that was pulling power away from his court.
To see why that mattered so much to him, you need to see his kingdom first.

### R3 · The Kingdom
Kongo's capital, Mbanza Kongo, stood on a hilltop in what is now northern Angola. It wasn't Kinshasa. And the kingdom wasn't the same thing as either of the two countries called Congo today, although its lands reached into parts of both.
By the late fourteen hundreds, Kongo was a large kingdom. The king appointed governors to run its provinces, from Soyo on the coast to the lands further inland.
Its kings weren't simply born into the job. A new king came from among the royal descendants, and the choice could be fought over.
People paid tribute and traded in cloth woven from raffia palm, in salt and in copper. Their money included nzimbu, small shells gathered from an island off the coast near Luanda.
So long before any European ship arrived, Kongo had a capital, a court, tribute, money and trade routes of its own. The Portuguese joined a world that was already organised.

### R4 · The Relationship
They arrived in the 1480s. Within a few years, Kongo and Portugal were exchanging envoys.
In 1491, King Nzinga a Nkuwu was baptised. One of his sons took the name Afonso.
When the old king died, around 1506, Afonso had to fight a rival from the royal family for the throne. He won, and he made Christianity part of how Kongo was ruled.
He wrote to the Pope and sent envoys to Rome. He sent young Kongolese to study in Lisbon. And in 1518, Pope Leo the Tenth made his son Henrique a bishop.
In 1516, a Portuguese priest, Rui de Aguiar, wrote home that Afonso knew the prophets and the Gospel better than the priests did, and that he often fell asleep over his books. That's a missionary praising a convert, so take it with a pinch of salt. But it also tells you schools were running, including one for girls, taught by Afonso's sister, who had learned to read in old age.
None of this came free. In 1512, Portugal's King Manuel told his envoy to make sure the ships came home loaded with "slaves, copper and ivory", to cover the cost of the priests and of educating Afonso's sons.
And Afonso paid. In a letter of 1514, he describes sending more than four hundred war captives to load a single Portuguese ship. In Kongo, people captured in war could be sold. Afonso used that rule.

### R5 · What He Objected To
So what had gone wrong by 1526?
Two things. The first was goods. Afonso wrote that Portuguese merchants were setting up shops and selling goods he'd banned. Those goods were spreading so widely that some of his lords now had more of them than he did, and they were turning away from his authority. For a king who held power by rewarding loyalty, that was dangerous.
The second was people. Kongolese who wanted those goods were kidnapping free people, including nobles and members of the king's own family, and selling them to the Portuguese.
In July, Afonso's answer was blunt. Send us priests and schoolteachers, he wrote, and wine and flour for the Mass, and nothing else. "It is our will," he says, "that in these kingdoms there should be no trade in slaves, nor any outlet for them."

### R6 · The October Law
Three months later, on the eighteenth of October, he wrote again. And this letter tells you more.
Free people were being taken in secret, some at night. Once the buyers had them, they were branded with hot irons. Afonso's guards caught some of them at the waterside, and the buyers couldn't say who had sold them.
So he made a law. Any white man buying people in Kongo had to show them first to three officials of the royal court: Dom Pedro Manipunzo, Dom Manuel Manissaba, and Gonçalo Pires. The three would decide whether each person was free or lawfully enslaved. If they passed, they could be shipped. If not, the buyer lost them.
That's not abolition. It's a king deciding who could be sold, and making sure the decision was his.

### R7 · Portugal'S Reply
So did Portugal listen? It did reply.
In a letter written towards the end of 1529, King João said he'd been told Kongo was so full of people that it seemed not a single slave had ever left it. He suggested a fixed place in the market, where two of Afonso's men would check who was being sold.
Then he added this. He'd agree, he said, to no slave trade at all, and send one small ship a year with wine and flour. But that wouldn't do Kongo any honour. Better, he wrote, that ten thousand slaves left Kongo every year, with ten thousand copper bracelets and as many ivory tusks.
The same letter told the Portuguese priests in Kongo how to have slaves bought for their own upkeep.
So the trade went on, and the Church was part of it. Afonso kept writing to Lisbon for years, and he died in the early 1540s.
There's a lot these letters can't tell us. They were written for a royal reader, by a king making his case. We have his side, and Lisbon's. We don't have the voices of the people who were taken.

### R8 · The Answer
So why did Kongo's king complain to Portugal about the slave trade?
Not because he thought slavery was wrong in the way we would today. He complained because the trade had slipped out of his hands. Free people, his nobles and his own relatives were being sold. Foreign goods were buying the loyalty of his lords. And he wanted the power to say who could be sold, and who couldn't.
What his letters show is a kingdom with its own laws, officials and courts, ruled by a king who read, argued and made laws. And they show how fast a trade in people can outgrow the people who think they control it.

## 4. Pronunciation guide (names that appear in the narration)
**Status: best evidence, not yet checked by a Kikongo speaker (gap G7).**
- Kikongo names start with a soft nasal ("m-", "n-") that runs into the next sound. Don't say "em" or "en" as a separate syllable.
- Portuguese names: use a natural English-speaker version; don't force full Portuguese nasals.

| Name | Say it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Kongo | KONG-go | The kingdom. Spelled with K to separate it from the modern countries |
| Mvemba a Nzinga | mVEM-bah ah nZING-gah | Afonso's Kikongo name. The "a" means "of" |
| Mbanza Kongo | mBAHN-zah KONG-go | The capital. "Mbanza" = town/capital |
| Nzinga a Nkuwu | nZING-gah ah nKOO-woo | Afonso's father |
| nzimbu | nZEEM-boo | Shell money |
| Soyo | SOY-oh | Coastal province and port (Portuguese "Sonho") |
| Luanda | loo-AN-dah | Angola's capital today |
| Kinshasa | kin-SHAH-sah | Said only to rule it out |
| Afonso | ah-FON-soh | Portuguese spelling, one "f" |
| João | zhoo-OW (approx.) | Full Portuguese is nasal; "zhoo-OW" is fine for an English narration |
| Dom João Teixeira | dom zhoo-OW tay-SHAY-rah | The secretary |
| Henrique | en-REE-keh | Afonso's son, the bishop |
| Rui de Aguiar | ROO-ee deh ah-gee-AR | The priest (hard "g") |
| Manuel | man-WELL | King Manuel I |
| Dom Pedro Manipunzo | dom PED-roh mah-nee-POON-zoh | Court official (Kongolese title-name) |
| Dom Manuel Manissaba | dom man-WELL mah-nee-SAH-bah | Chief bailiff |
| Gonçalo Pires | gon-SAH-loo PEE-resh | Chief outfitter |
| Leo the Tenth | as written | Pope Leo X |
