Four companies are about to spend more than six hundred billion dollars in a single year on buildings full of computers. Not on apps. Not on adverts. On concrete, chips, cables and power. If you're building a portfolio for the long run, that's worth understanding. So in the next five minutes, let's look at who gets paid when an AI data centre gets built, and what could go wrong.

First, the scale. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta have each pointed to roughly one hundred and twenty-five to two hundred billion dollars of capital spending for twenty twenty-six. Added together, estimates range from about six hundred and fifty to seven hundred and sixty billion, depending on the source and the date. In twenty twenty-five it was around four hundred billion. That's close to double in a year. Part of that rise is simply chips getting more expensive. But either way, this money is going out of the door now, and it lands in somebody else's revenue.

Why so much? Because AI runs on electricity. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centres used about four hundred and fifteen terawatt-hours in twenty twenty-four, around one and a half percent of the world's power. Its central forecast is roughly nine hundred and forty-five by twenty thirty. That's about double, heading toward three percent of global electricity. The same report warned that about a fifth of planned data centres could face delays getting connected to the grid. So power isn't a side issue. It's the bottleneck.

So who gets paid? Think in layers, like a supply chain. Layer one: the chips. The processors that do the thinking, and the memory that feeds them. Layer two: networking. The cables and switches that join thousands of chips into one machine. Layer three: power and cooling. Electrical equipment, backup systems, and the cooling that stops it all overheating. Layer four: the buildings, and the landlords who own them. Some listed property companies do exactly this. And layer five: the spenders, the big tech companies paying for all of it.

Notice the difference. The first four layers earn money when the spending happens. The fifth layer only earns if the spending pays back later. That's why people call the first four the picks and shovels.

It's happening here too. The UK government has been designating what it calls AI Growth Zones, starting at Culham in Oxfordshire, then the North East of England, South Wales, North Wales and Scotland. The aim is sites of five hundred megawatts or more, with faster planning and power. The investment totals announced, in the tens of billions, are projections, not guaranteed spending. Treat headlines as intentions, and wait for the building work and the revenue to show up.

Now the honest part. Every infrastructure boom carries one big risk: overbuilding. If AI demand grows slower than the spending, orders for suppliers can fall quickly, and share prices tend to fall even faster. There's pressure on the spenders too. Amazon's trailing free cash flow dropped sharply as it spent, and when Meta raised its spending plans, its shares fell about six percent. Markets are asking whether this pays back. And there's concentration. Many of these companies are already big holdings inside a global tracker fund. So adding a data centre theme on top can mean owning the same names twice.

For a UK investor, the practical questions are simple. How would I get exposure: single shares, or a diversified UCITS fund? What does it cost each year? How much does it overlap with what I already hold? And does it sit inside a Stocks and Shares ISA, where growth stays free of UK tax? A theme should be a small slice of a plan, sized so that if it halves, your long-term goal is still intact.
