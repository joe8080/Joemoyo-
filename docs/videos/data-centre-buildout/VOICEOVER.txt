More than seven hundred billion dollars. That's roughly what Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta plan to spend on AI infrastructure this year, close to double last year. If you invest for the long run, you don't need to guess which company wins. You need five questions you can check every quarter. Let's go through them.

Question one. Is the spending turning into orders? Spending only matters to an investor if it lands as someone else's revenue. Right now it is. The big chip makers report data-centre revenue growing somewhere between one hundred and two hundred percent a year, while the spenders' capital budgets are growing at around eighty percent. Orders are keeping pace with the cheques. That's the picks and shovels argument, and for now the numbers support it.

Question two. Can the chips actually be built? Yes, but tightly. The memory that feeds AI chips is sold out into twenty twenty-seven, and the advanced packaging that joins chips together is sold out through this year. One chip maker has said supply will cap next year's growth at around seventy percent, while demand is nearer double that. A shortage helps the suppliers' pricing, but it also means projects wait in a queue.

Question three, and the one that matters most. Do the buildings have power? I track one hundred and six AI data centre sites. Between them, about seventy-seven gigawatts are planned. Only about eight gigawatts are running today. And only around thirty percent of the capacity due in the next twelve months has documented, secured power. It's already biting. One five-gigawatt campus has pushed its first stage back by about two years. Another is roughly seven months behind after a gas pipeline was refused. In the UK, five sites are tracked, around six hundred and seventy megawatts on paper, and none is running yet. A building without power is a very expensive car park. The International Energy Agency expects data centres to roughly double their electricity use by twenty thirty, to about three percent of the world's power, and warns that around a fifth of planned sites could face grid delays.

Question four. Is the electrical and cooling equipment keeping up? Every maker reporting orders against sales is above one point two, and lead times are still getting longer. That tells you demand is ahead of supply. The thing to watch is any maker whose orders go quiet, because that's usually the first crack.

Question five, the uncomfortable one. Is AI paying for itself? Not yet. Cloud revenue is growing around forty percent. Capital spending is growing nearer eighty. The biggest cloud providers report order backlogs of around five hundred billion dollars each, which tells you customers have signed up. But a backlog is a promise, not cash. That gap is being filled with borrowing, share issuance and clever structures. One giant has raised around a hundred billion dollars of debt. Another reported almost no free cash flow in its latest quarter. Another moved a data centre into a joint venture, so the debt sits outside its own balance sheet. And some of the money is circular: a company invests in an AI lab, and the lab spends it back on that company's cloud. None of that is unusual, but it's also how past booms were financed, so you should know.

Before the takeaway, here's where the money lands. Chips and memory get paid first. Then networking and optical links, which join thousands of chips into one machine. Then the factories and packaging lines that make the chips. Then the power and cooling equipment, the transformers, switchgear and chillers. Then the builders and landlords who own the buildings. Then power generation, including nuclear and gas, and the materials underneath it all, from copper to rare earths. Each layer is paid when the spending happens. The spenders at the top are the only ones who have to wait for the payback.

So what does a long-term UK investor do with this? Three things. One, remember the layers: chips, memory, networking, power equipment, buildings, and the spenders. Two, notice that the tight layers, right now power and memory, tend to hold the pricing power. Three, size it. A theme like this is volatile, many of these companies are already inside a global tracker fund, and the answer to what if I'm wrong should be a small slice, ideally in a Stocks and Shares ISA, with UCITS funds where you want diversification.

Five questions: orders, chips, power, equipment, payback. Check them every quarter, not every day. This is education, not advice, and I may hold some of the companies in this theme. If this helped, subscribe, and I'll see you in the next one.
